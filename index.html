An AI feature that isn’t allowed to create anything

Optevo · Product Manager, Platform and AI · 2026

Teams were reading long briefs and discussion threads and retyping them into tasks by hand — slow, inconsistent, and the reason a task existed was lost the moment someone created it. The obvious fix is to let an AI create the tasks. That’s the version most teams build, and it’s the wrong one.

My call
The AI proposes; it never creates. Proposals land in a holding state, a permitted user reviews, edits, rejects or approves, and only on approval does the existing task API create anything — tagged with its source and marked AI-assisted. The AI service has no ability to create, mutate, approve or convert a task.
Under pressure
Timeline risk surfaced two days into the build window. I took engineering’s compression proposal, wrote up what it preserved and what it gave up, and put it to the decision-maker as an explicit trade-off. Scope moved; the committed date held.
Shipped
On the committed date. Ten user stories passing with captured verification evidence, four release blockers and three polish items closed with retest proof, and an independent QA re-test of nineteen items on top of primary QA.
Read the full case study

Generating work items by hand is a quiet, expensive problem. Someone reads a brief or a long thread, decides what needs doing, and retypes it as tasks. It is slow and inconsistent, and the link back to why a task exists disappears at the moment of creation — so nobody can later answer why something is on the board.

The obvious solution is to let a model create the tasks. I argued against it. That design drops unreviewed model output straight into a team’s real work queue, with no accountability for what it made or where it came from. So I locked a different governance model before any build started: the AI writes reviewable proposals into a holding state, a permitted user reviews and edits, rejects or approves, and only approval triggers creation through the existing task API. The AI service itself can neither create, mutate, approve nor convert a task. AI-assisted, not AI-executed.

Two days into the build window, timeline risk surfaced. Rather than ask for more time against a leadership-committed date, I took engineering’s compression proposal — build only the two genuinely new screens, reuse the proven task-creation modal for review and approval — documented what it preserved and what it sacrificed, and put it up as an explicit trade-off rather than a decision already made.

When the hardening pass produced failures, I confirmed four scope decisions and wrote the reasoning for each: a source anchor deferred as post-release, image sources out of scope, release scope fixed at exactly two source paths so QA had a stable baseline, attachments deferred. I cut scope, but I refused to cut the honest error message — unsupported content had to say so plainly rather than fail silently. I then wrote the release-readiness evidence: every story with its verification, every blocker with an owner and proof of closure, five known limitations named and parked. I signalled readiness; the go/no-go call itself sat with the product owner.

Two months from initiative start to production, with a two-week build-to-release window. Distributed team across three timezones — product, design, backend, AI, two frontend engineers and two QA.

What this shows I would rather ship a smaller feature with a defensible boundary than a larger one that quietly moves authority away from the people accountable for the work.

A $1.1 billion rebate that barely moved the debt book

AGL Energy · Commercial Analyst, Credit & Affordability · 2024

A large government energy rebate was landing on customer accounts. AGL needed to know whether that cash would actually pay down its debt book — or whether chronic and partial payers would absorb it without changing behaviour at all.

My call
I argued for Monte Carlo simulation over a single-point forecast. Customer response to a rebate is variable, and a single number hides exactly the range that matters for provisioning. I also ran bootstrapping to estimate debt ratios for the partial and zero-payer segments, which had the thinnest direct evidence.
Built
A segment transition model and a segment write-off model across 25 customer segments, forecasting to end of FY25. I named my own simplifying assumption — that segment behaviour holds steady across the year — as a stated limitation rather than burying it.
Found
A forecast reduction of roughly 2% in the debt book. The interesting result is the direction, not the size: a very large rebate produced only a modest improvement, which is the opposite of the intuitive answer and the reason the work mattered.
Read the full case study

The commercial stake was bad-debt provisioning, collections strategy and the FY25 debt position. If the rebate did the work, collections effort could be redirected elsewhere. If it didn’t, the write-off assumptions on the book were wrong. The prevailing view treated the rebate’s effect as more uniform across customers than it actually was, and behaviour differed sharply between chronic, partial and zero payers.

My contribution was the method. I argued for Monte Carlo simulation rather than a single-point forecast, on the grounds that a point estimate hides the distribution — and for a provisioning decision, the spread is the thing you actually need. For the segments with the least direct evidence, I used bootstrapping over historical data to estimate debt ratios rather than assuming them.

The output was a segment transition model and a segment write-off model, feeding a team report and a final presentation delivered on site to the AGL business sponsor. My model results were referenced directly in the report’s conclusions. Five weeks, team of four.

The forecast came back at roughly a 2% reduction against a rebate worth $1.1 billion. Stated plainly: this is a forecast, not a realised outcome, and I have no visibility into whether the recommendation was adopted. The value was in the direction of the finding — that a very large intervention was likely to produce a modest movement — and in showing the range around it rather than a single reassuring number.

What this shows I pick the method that answers the actual decision, and I state the limitations of my own model before someone else has to find them.

Designing for the farmer who doesn’t own a phone

Team UAS NMIMS · Techfest, IIT Bombay · 2020

Nearly every agri-tech product assumes a smartphone. A large share of the wheat cultivators we were designing for don’t own one. That single constraint changed the hardware, not just the interface.

My role
Shaped the solution, planned the build and ran the timeline across a student engineering team, designed the UI flow for the marketplace, and wrote the technical report and rationale the entry was judged on. The hardware build sat with teammates.
Scoped
Deliberately narrowed to wheat across four contiguous states, and wrote down why: India is the second-largest wheat cultivator globally, and those states cover a large share of both irrigated and non-irrigated production.
Built
A solar-powered field sensor unit on a custom PCB with eight sensor types, a handheld spot-check device with its own built-in screen, and a deployed web marketplace with a sell flow letting farmers set their own price.
Read the full case study

Indian agriculture supports around 58% of the population, and the farmers we looked at were making sowing and irrigation decisions from experience rather than measurement — no reliable read on soil contamination, nutrient levels or water quality, and no direct route to market.

The constraint that shaped everything was that a large share of these farmers do not own a phone. That is why the handheld unit carries its own built-in screen instead of pairing to a mobile app. Compounding it: users who are illiterate or semi-literate and not market-savvy, so the output had to be readable at a glance, on a device you own, without text comprehension. The screen is a single glanceable panel — nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and moisture on one side, temperature, humidity and light on the other.

The marketplace piece was a response to a live policy change. The 2020 ordinance let buyers purchase outside the regulated mandis, removing the guaranteed-price protection farmers had relied on. The forum exists because that protection disappeared — registration and login, a dashboard, a shop, a sell flow where farmers set their own price against a base price, and a community discussion space. I designed that flow.

We built three components rather than a concept deck: a solar-powered field unit in a PVC housing with a custom circuit soldered onto a PCB around a Node MCU, carrying camera, temperature, humidity, sunlight index, NPK, moisture and pH sensors — solar because battery replacement in a field isn’t realistic; an Arduino Nano handheld for spot checks; and the deployed web application. A specified ML pipeline on Flask handled yield, price and watering-cycle prediction, trained on gridded surface meteorological data with covariates reduced to principal components.

Fifth nationally at Techfest, IIT Bombay. Honest framing: this was a functioning prototype producing live sensor readings, not a calibrated, field-validated system, and the ML pipeline was specified against simulated data rather than measured in the field. It did not go further than the competition.

What this shows I find the constraint that everyone else designs around, and let it change the product rather than the marketing.
