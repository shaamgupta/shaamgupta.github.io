Turning documents into tasks, without handing over control

Optevo · Product Manager, Platform and AI · 2026

AI governance design Release readiness Scope negotiation QA triage Azure DevOps Jira Figma

People were reading long briefs and discussion threads and retyping them into tasks by hand. It is slow, and something quietly goes missing along the way. Once a task has been typed out, nothing connects it back to where it came from, so a few weeks later nobody can really say why it is on the board.

Context
The obvious fix is to let an AI create the tasks. That is the version most teams build. I was not comfortable with it, because it puts unreviewed model output straight into a team’s real work queue with nobody accountable for what it made.
What I did
Locked a different shape before anything was built. The AI writes proposals into a holding state. Someone with the right permissions reviews, edits, rejects or approves. Only on approval does the existing task API create anything, tagged with where it came from. The AI service cannot create or change a task at all.
What happened
It shipped on the date leadership had committed to. Ten user stories passing with evidence captured, four blockers and three polish items closed with retest proof, and an independent QA pass of nineteen items on top of the primary one.
Read the whole story

Generating work items by hand is one of those problems nobody complains about loudly, because it just feels like the job. Someone reads a brief or a long thread, works out what needs doing, and retypes it as tasks. It is slow and it comes out differently depending on who did it. The part that bothered me more was the traceability. The moment a task is typed out by hand, the link back to the conversation it came from is gone, and a manager looking at the board later has no way to answer why something is on it.

So the case for using AI here was easy. The question was what the AI should be allowed to do, and that is where I think a lot of teams are getting it wrong right now. If the model creates the tasks directly, you have put something nobody reviewed into the queue that people are actually working from, and there is no one to point at when it is wrong.

I settled the shape before the build started. The AI writes reviewable proposals into a holding state. A permitted user reviews them, edits them, rejects or approves them. Only approval triggers creation, and it goes through the existing task API rather than a new path, tagged with its source and marked as AI-assisted. The AI service itself has no ability to create, change, approve or convert a task. Assisted, not executed.

Two days into the build window a timeline risk surfaced. The date had been committed by leadership, so rather than go back and ask for more time I took the compression option engineering suggested, which was to build only the two genuinely new screens and reuse the existing task creation modal for review and approval. I wrote up what that kept and what it gave up, and put it to the decision maker as a trade-off rather than something already decided.

When the hardening pass turned up failures I confirmed four scope calls and wrote down the reasoning for each. A source anchor moved to post-release. Image sources out of scope. Release scope fixed at two source paths so QA had something stable to test against. Attachments deferred. The one thing I would not cut was the error message: if a user gave it something unsupported, the interface had to say so plainly rather than fail quietly.

I wrote the release readiness evidence, every story with its verification, every blocker with an owner and proof it was closed, five known limitations named and parked rather than quietly shipped. I signalled that it was ready. The go/no-go call itself sat with the product owner.

Two months from starting the initiative to production, with a two week build window at the end. Product, design, backend, AI, two frontend engineers and two QA, across three timezones.

The thing I keep coming back to is that the smaller version was the better one. We gave up a fair bit of scope to hold the date, and almost none of it turned out to matter.

What a $1.1 billion rebate actually did to the debt book

AGL Energy · Commercial Analyst, Credit and Affordability · 2024

Monte Carlo simulation Bootstrapping Segment transition modelling Debt forecasting Python Excel

A large government energy rebate was about to land on customer accounts. The question AGL needed answered was whether that money would genuinely pay down its debt book, or whether customers who were already struggling would absorb it without their payment behaviour changing at all.

Context
It mattered for bad-debt provisioning and for where collections effort should go. If the rebate did the work, that resource could be pointed somewhere else. If it did not, the write-off assumptions on the book were wrong. The prevailing view treated the effect as more even across customers than it turned out to be.
What I did
Argued for Monte Carlo simulation instead of a single-point forecast. Customer response to a rebate varies a lot, and a single number hides the range, which is exactly the thing a provisioning decision needs. For the segments with the least evidence behind them I used bootstrapping over historical data rather than assuming a figure.
What happened
The forecast came back at roughly a 2% reduction, across 25 customer segments out to the end of FY25. Five weeks, team of four. My results were referenced in the report’s conclusions and presented on site to the AGL sponsor.
Read the whole story

The rebate was worth $1.1 billion, and the intuitive answer is that money that large has to move the debt book meaningfully. What we found was that it probably would not. Roughly 2%. That gap between what you would assume and what the numbers suggested is the actual finding, and it is the part I found genuinely interesting.

My contribution was mostly the method. A single-point forecast would have given the business one number to plan around, and for a provisioning decision that is close to useless, because what you actually need to know is how wrong the number could be. So I made the case for Monte Carlo simulation, which gives you the spread rather than a single reassuring figure. For the partial and zero payer segments, where there was very little direct evidence to work from, I resampled historical data to estimate the debt ratios instead of picking something plausible.

I also made a simplifying assumption I was not entirely happy with, which was that segment behaviour holds steady across the year and does not fluctuate month to month. It was the right call for the time we had, but I wrote it into the report as a stated limitation rather than leaving it for someone else to find.

The output was a segment transition model and a segment write-off model, feeding into a team report and a final presentation delivered on site to the AGL business sponsor. To be straightforward about the limits of this: it is a forecast, not a realised outcome, and I have no visibility into whether the recommendation was picked up afterwards.

This is the project that moved me from analytics into product. The modelling was good fun, but the part I kept thinking about afterwards was what anyone was supposed to do with the answer.

Building for farmers who do not own a phone

Team UAS NMIMS · Techfest, IIT Bombay · 2020

Product discovery UI flow design Embedded sensing Python Flask Arduino / Node MCU Heroku

Almost every agri-tech product assumes the farmer has a smartphone. A large share of the wheat cultivators we were designing for do not. Once we understood that properly, it changed the hardware, not just the screen.

Context
Farmers were making sowing and irrigation decisions from experience rather than measurement, with no reliable read on soil contamination, nutrient levels or water quality, and no direct route to market. Many of them were also illiterate or semi-literate, so anything we built had to be readable at a glance.
What I did
Shaped the solution, planned the build and ran the timeline across a student engineering team, designed the interface flow for the marketplace, and wrote the technical report and the rationale the entry was judged on. The hardware build sat with my teammates.
What happened
Fifth nationally at Techfest, IIT Bombay. We built a solar-powered field sensor unit on a custom PCB, a handheld spot-check device with its own built-in screen, and a deployed web marketplace where farmers set their own price.
Read the whole story

Indian agriculture supports around 58% of the population, and the farmers we were looking at were making decisions the way their families always had, from experience rather than measurement. There was no reliable way to know what was happening in the soil, and no direct route to a buyer.

The constraint that ended up shaping everything was that a lot of these farmers do not own a phone. It sounds obvious once you say it, but nearly every product in this space quietly assumes a smartphone sitting in someone’s pocket. It is why the handheld unit carries its own built-in screen instead of pairing to an app, and why that screen is laid out as one glanceable panel rather than a menu you have to navigate: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and moisture on one side, temperature, humidity and light on the other.

We narrowed to wheat across four contiguous states, and I wrote down why rather than leaving it as a convenience. India is the second largest wheat cultivator in the world, and those states cover a large share of both irrigated and non-irrigated production, which meant we could actually serve the thing we were scoping.

The marketplace piece came out of a policy change happening at the time. The 2020 ordinance allowed buyers to purchase outside the regulated mandis, which removed the guaranteed-price protection farmers had been relying on, and was being protested while we were working. The forum exists because that protection went away. It has registration and login, a dashboard, a shop, a sell flow where farmers set their own price against a base price, and a discussion space. I designed that flow.

We built three things rather than presenting a concept. A solar-powered field unit in a PVC housing, with a custom circuit soldered onto a PCB around a Node MCU, carrying camera, temperature, humidity, sunlight index, NPK, moisture and pH sensors. Solar, because expecting someone to replace a battery out in a field is not realistic. An Arduino Nano handheld for spot checks. And the web application. A specified ML pipeline on Flask handled yield, price and watering cycle prediction.

Being honest about what this was: a working prototype producing live sensor readings, not a calibrated or field-validated system, and the ML pipeline was specified against simulated data rather than measured out in a field. It did not go any further than the competition.

I was nineteen and this is the first time I remember a single fact about the people we were building for changing the whole shape of the thing. I have been looking for that fact first ever since.
