PROJECT 01 OF 03 My work at Optevo · 2026

An AI that drafts your tasks but is never allowed to create them

My role: Product Manager, Platform and AI. Product, design, backend, AI, two frontend engineers and two QA, across three timezones.

The problem

Teams were turning long briefs and discussion threads into work items by hand. Someone reads the document, decides what needs doing, and retypes it as tasks. It is slow, it comes out differently depending on who did it, and the link back to why a task exists disappears the moment it is created. Weeks later, nobody on the board can answer why something is on it.

My approach

The obvious build is to let the model write straight into the work queue, and I argued against it: that puts something nobody reviewed into the list people are actually working from. So I drew a hard line instead. The AI proposes, a person approves, and only then does the platform create anything. Try to break it below.

PROJECT 02 OF 03 My work at AGL Energy · 2024

Forecasting what a $1.1 billion rebate would do to the debt book

My role: Commercial Analyst, Credit and Affordability. Team of four, five weeks.

The problem

A $1.1 billion government energy rebate was about to land on customer accounts. AGL needed to know whether that money would genuinely pay down its debt book, or whether customers already struggling would absorb it without their payment behaviour changing at all. The answer drives how much bad debt to provision for and where to point collections effort.

My approach

A single-point forecast gives the business one number to plan around, which for a provisioning decision is close to the least useful shape. What you need is how wrong that number could be. So I argued for Monte Carlo simulation over 25 customer segments, and bootstrapped the segments where the evidence was thinnest rather than assuming a figure.

PROJECT 03 OF 03 My work with Team UAS NMIMS · Techfest, IIT Bombay · 2020

One assumption, and the whole thing changes shape

My role: shaped the solution, ran the timeline, designed the marketplace flow, wrote the report. The hardware build sat with my teammates.

The problem

Wheat farmers across four Indian states were making sowing and irrigation decisions from experience rather than measurement, with no reliable read on soil nutrients, moisture or water quality, and no direct route to a buyer. Most of them were also illiterate or semi-literate, and a large share did not own a phone.

My approach

Almost every product in this space quietly assumes a smartphone in someone's pocket. Taking that away is what shaped everything: the screen had to come back onto the device, and the readings had to be legible at a glance without reading text. Flip the assumption below and watch what has to change.

◎ Press either one

The constraint that changed the hardware

Once the phone goes, the screen has to come back to the device, and the readings have to be legible at a glance to someone who may not read at all. That is why the handheld carries its own display, laid out as one panel rather than a menu.

We narrowed to wheat across four contiguous states and I wrote down why. The marketplace piece came out of the 2020 farm ordinances, which opened up trade outside the regulated mandis and left many farmers worried the price floor they had relied on would erode in practice. I designed that flow.

Honest about what it was: a working prototype producing live sensor readings, not a calibrated or field-validated system. Fifth nationally. It did not go further.

The design sketch, 2020. Drawn before any of it existed.

Where the constraint shows up

On the right of the sketch, the handheld is annotated display for instructions. The screen was on the device from the first drawing, not bolted on later once we noticed the problem.

I am not a hardware engineer and did not build it. I drew what it needed to do and wrote the case for why.

02 – Research

Two peer-reviewed papers

On both I built the models, worked out the analytical rationale, and wrote most of the paper.

Brain Tumor Classification Using Deep Learning Techniques

Mahajan, Gupta, Bhanushali & Joshi · 2024
Lecture Notes in Networks and Systems, vol. 818, pp. 15–34 · Springer · doi:10.1007/978-981-99-7862-5_2

Abstract

A review and comparison of deep learning architectures for classifying benign, malignant and pituitary brain tumours, weighing traditional CNN approaches against experimental ones, and looking at where the technique could reduce how often a biopsy is needed.

Body Pose Estimation using Deep Learning

Mahajan, Gupta & Bhanushali · 2023
IJRASET, 11(3), pp. 1419–1424 · doi:10.22214/ijraset.2023.49688

Abstract

A real-time human pose detection and recognition system built on MediaPipe, identifying body poses from both live and recorded video streams. The paper walks through the pipeline end to end: extracting skeletal landmarks frame by frame, deriving joint angles from those landmarks, and classifying the resulting posture without the depth camera or wearable sensors most comparable systems rely on. We looked at where it holds up and where it does not, including partial occlusion, unusual camera angles and low frame rates, and at the applications that follow from running on an ordinary webcam: physiotherapy and rehabilitation feedback, sports and form analysis, gesture interfaces and fall detection.
