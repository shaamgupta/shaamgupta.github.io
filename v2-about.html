about me

I came into product from analytics, and the thing that moved me across was noticing that the model was never the interesting part. What people did with the output was.

At AGL I spent five weeks building a simulation to forecast what a $1.1 billion rebate would do to a debt book. The modelling was good fun. The part I kept turning over afterwards was what anyone was supposed to do with the answer, and who would have to decide.

That question is roughly my job now. I work on AI and platform features for an AI-driven SaaS product, which mostly means sitting between what a team wants to build and what it can honestly say is ready to ship.

How I work

Three things that tend to be true

Not principles I came up with in the abstract. They are what I notice myself doing when a project gets difficult.

I write it down

Most of the arguments I have been in were not about the decision. They were about what the decision actually was. I would rather spend an hour writing the thing down badly and let the team correct it than have everyone leave the room agreeing to different plans.

Smaller, with the boundary intact

When a date is at risk I would rather cut scope than cut the part that makes it defensible. On the AI feature I gave up a fair amount of what we had planned, and almost none of it turned out to matter. What I would not give up was the error message that told a user honestly when we could not handle their file.

I state my own limits first

My AGL model assumed customer behaviour held steady across the year, which is not true. I wrote it into the report as a limitation rather than waiting for someone to find it. A number is easier to trust when you know what it cannot do.

Before product

Things I started

I co-founded and ran AbeerGulaal Foundation for about four years, a menstrual health nonprofit working across several Indian states. We built a network of more than two hundred volunteers with no paid staff and no office, and I made thirteen field visits to communities we were trying to serve, largely because I did not trust what I thought I knew about them from a distance.

Before that I started a Google Developer Student Club from nothing and grew it to around seven hundred and fifty students across three states, running twenty-two workshops and raising the funding to keep them going.

Neither is product work. Both taught me the same thing the field visits did, which is that you find out what people need by going and asking, and that the version you imagined at your desk is usually wrong in a way you could not have guessed.
